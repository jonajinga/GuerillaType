The office of the affluent chief inspector was a difficult place to find.
Fifty flights of stairs, a waffle of corridors, and an efficient clerk who insisted that the file had been reclassified before the affidavit was ever notified.
He offered no explanation, only a shrug and a sufficiently official form, which he flourished at me twice.
"The difficulty," he said, "is that the office was notified after the fact, and the fact itself was filed elsewhere."
It was the sort of sentence that made the afternoon feel longer than it had any right to be.
Outside, the fog was lifting off the river and the traffic was beginning to thin.
A woman selling flowers had set up beneath the scaffolding, and the effect was almost cheerful.
She offered me a daffodil for fifteen pence and I took it without arguing, because arguing had already cost me the afternoon.
Vast quantities of paper move through that building every day--forms, affidavits, certificates, and the occasional genuine document--and almost none of it ever reaches the person it concerns.
The clerk told me that 12 500 files had been reclassified that quarter, which he seemed to regard as an achievement rather than a confession.
Walking away, I thought about how efficiently an office can be organised around the task of not answering a question.
The flowers were still there when I passed again at five, and the woman was still waiting, and the scaffolding had not moved at all.
